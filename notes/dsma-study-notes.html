SECTION 01

📐 Foundations

The language every model speaks. Master this and everything else clicks into place.

1.1 What is a Variable? Types of Data

Everything in data science starts with data — and data comes in different types. The type of data you have determines which models you can use.

💡 You cannot average city names. You cannot find the "median" of Amsterdam, Berlin, Paris. Matching your model to your data type is the first decision you make every single time.

Why this matters for your career: when a client gives you a dataset, your first question is always "what types are these variables?" It determines everything — which model to use, how to visualise it, how to interpret it.

1.2 The Normal Distribution

Many things in nature follow a bell curve: heights, test scores, customer spending, measurement errors. Most models assume your data looks like this.

  • Mean (μ): The centre — the average value
  • Standard deviation (σ): How spread out the data is. Small σ = tightly packed. Large σ = widely spread.
  • 68–95–99.7 rule: 68% of data falls within 1σ of the mean. 95% within 2σ. 99.7% within 3σ.
Real example: Average order value is €50, σ = €10. That means 68% of orders are between €40–€60. An order of €120 is extremely unusual — worth investigating for fraud or a data error.

1.3 Correlation vs. Causation

One of the most important concepts in all of data science. Getting it wrong leads to catastrophically bad business decisions.

ConceptDefinitionExample
CorrelationTwo things move togetherIce cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer
CausationX actually causes Y to changeSmoking causes lung cancer — proven by controlled experiments
ConfoundA hidden third variable causes bothHot weather causes both ice cream sales AND more swimming
Business trap: Your data shows customers who open email newsletters buy more. Does the newsletter cause purchases — or do already-engaged customers both open emails AND buy more regardless? You cannot tell from correlation alone. This is why A/B testing exists.

1.4 Probability — The Language of Uncertainty

Every model outputs probabilities. Probability = how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

SituationProbability
A fair coin lands heads0.5 (50%)
Rolling a 6 on a die0.167 (16.7%)
A customer churns this month0.23 (23%)

Conditional probability — probably the most useful concept in marketing analytics:

P(buy | opened email) = probability a customer buys, GIVEN they opened the email If P(buy) = 10% for all customers But P(buy | opened email) = 35% → Focus your ad spend on email openers.

1.5 The Bias-Variance Trade-off

The central tension in all of machine learning. Every model decision you make is navigating this.

  • Bias: Model assumptions too simplistic — misses real patterns
  • Variance: Model too sensitive — learned noise, fails on new data
  • Goal: Capture real patterns AND generalise to unseen data
Analogy: Memorising past exam questions vs. understanding the subject. Memorising = high variance (you fail new questions). Ignoring everything = high bias (you fail everything). Understanding = the sweet spot.

1.6 What is a Model, Really?

At its core, a model is a mathematical function that takes inputs and produces an output:

Input (features) → Model → Output (prediction) Example: Predict customer lifetime value Inputs: age, number of purchases, average order value, days since last visit Output: predicted spend next 12 months The model learns the relationship between inputs and outputs from historical data. That learning process is called training.

SECTION 02

🎯 Supervised Learning — Core

You have labelled data (you know the answer). You want the model to learn and predict.

2.1 Linear Regression

Find the straight line (or flat plane) that best fits your data to predict a numerical outcome.

The line is defined by two things:

  • Intercept: Where the line crosses the Y axis — baseline value when inputs = 0
  • Slope (coefficient): How much Y changes for every 1-unit increase in X
Real example: Revenue = 500 + 3.2 × (ad spend)
For every extra €1 on ads, revenue goes up €3.20. The intercept (€500) is baseline revenue with no ads at all.

Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net

These are penalised versions of linear regression that prevent overfitting by adding a punishment for complexity.

MethodWhat it doesWhen to use
RidgeShrinks all coefficients toward zero, keeps all variablesMany variables, all somewhat relevant
LassoShrinks some coefficients to exactly zero — removes variablesYou want automatic feature selection
Elastic NetMix of Ridge + LassoBoth scenarios apply at once
Analogy: Predicting house price with 100 features. Lasso might say "only 12 of these actually matter" and zero out the rest. Ridge keeps all 100 but shrinks them.

When to use

  • Outcome is a continuous number: price, revenue, temperature, clicks
  • You want to understand which factors drive the outcome and by how much
  • You want an interpretable model a manager can read and trust
⚠️ Watch out for: Assumes a straight-line relationship. Outliers drag the line. Fails with highly correlated features (multicollinearity).

2.2 Logistic Regression

Despite the name, this is a classification model. It fits an S-shaped sigmoid curve that maps inputs to a probability between 0 and 1.

Output: a probability (e.g., 0.73 = 73% chance this customer churns). You pick a threshold — usually 0.5 — to make the final yes/no decision.

When to use

  • Binary outcomes: churn/no churn, click/no click, buy/no buy, fraud/not fraud
  • You want a probability score for ranking, not just a label
  • You need an interpretable model with clear feature importance
⚠️ Watch out for: Class imbalance — if only 1% of customers churn, the model will struggle. Always check your class distribution first.

2.3 Decision Trees

A model that splits data into groups using a series of yes/no questions — exactly like a flowchart. The tree learns which questions to ask and in which order from the data.

How it learns: At each split, the tree tries every possible question and picks the one that best separates the groups, measured by Gini impurity or Information Gain — both measure how "mixed" a group is after the split. Purer groups = better split.
Real example: A Netflix churn-prediction tree might split on: days since last watch → number of profiles → subscription tier → age. The model automatically finds the most powerful questions.

When to use

  • You want a model humans can read and explain to stakeholders ("white box")
  • Mix of numerical and categorical features
  • Quick first model to understand your data
⚠️ Watch out for: Very prone to overfitting. Small data changes create a completely different tree. This is why we almost always use them inside Random Forests in practice.

2.4 Support Vector Machines (SVM)

Finds the widest possible margin (street) between two classes of data. The decision boundary sits in the middle of that margin.

The kernel trick: Real data is rarely separable by a straight line. SVMs mathematically transform data into a higher dimension where a straight hyperplane can separate the classes.

Analogy: Two groups of dots mixed on a table. You can't draw a straight line between them. But if you lift some dots into 3D, a flat sheet of paper can now separate them. That's the kernel trick.

When to use

  • Text classification: spam detection, sentiment analysis
  • Small-to-medium datasets with clear class boundaries
  • High-dimensional data (many features)
⚠️ Watch out for: Slow on very large datasets. Hard to interpret. Sensitive to feature scaling — always normalise your data first.

SECTION 03

🌲 Ensemble & Advanced ML

What if instead of one model, you used hundreds? That is the power of ensemble methods.

3.1 Random Forest

Build hundreds of decision trees, each trained on a random subset of data and features. Final prediction = majority vote (classification) or average (regression).

Why better than one tree? Each tree makes different mistakes because it saw different data. When you average them, mistakes cancel out. This is variance reduction.

Analogy: Ask 500 people to guess the weight of a cow. Average their guesses — it's almost always more accurate than any single person. That's the "wisdom of crowds" — and the exact intuition behind Random Forests.

Feature importance

Random forests tell you which features mattered most across all trees. Hugely valuable in marketing: "Which customer attributes best predict purchase likelihood?"

When to use

  • Almost any classification or regression problem as a strong baseline
  • Messy real-world data with outliers and missing values
  • When you need feature importance to guide business decisions

3.2 Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)

Build trees sequentially — each new tree specifically corrects the errors of the previous one. Unlike Random Forest (trees built independently), every tree in gradient boosting knows what the last one got wrong.

The full step-by-step process — using the example above

The worked example: predicting weight (kg) from Height, Favourite Colour, and Gender. The tree is NOT built on weight directly — it is built on the residuals (errors) from the previous prediction.

Residual = Actual value − Current prediction Person 1: actual weight 88kg, current prediction 71.2kg → residual = +16.8 (underestimated) Person 3: actual weight 56kg, current prediction 71.2kg → residual = −15.2 (overestimated) The next tree's job: predict these residuals using the input features.
  • Step 1 — Try every possible split on every feature. For each candidate split, calculate the Sum of Squared Residuals (SSR) in each resulting group. Lower SSR = more similar residuals within the group = better split.
  • Step 2 — Pick the split with the lowest total SSR. In the example, splitting on Gender gives the lowest SSR (774.8), so Gender becomes the root node.
  • Step 3 — Keep splitting up to a max depth. In gradient boosting, trees are deliberately kept shallow — typically max depth 2–4. This prevents overfitting. A depth-2 tree might then split Males on Height and Females on Colour.
  • Step 4 — Each leaf outputs the average of its residuals. Male + Height < 1.7 leaf: average of 16.8 and 5.8 = +11.3. Female + Blue leaf: average of −15.2 and −14.2 = −14.7.
  • Step 5 — Update predictions using a learning rate. New prediction = old prediction + (learning rate × leaf value).
  • Step 6 — Calculate new residuals. Build the next tree on those. Repeat hundreds of times.
Why shallow trees and a small learning rate?

If you used deep trees, the model would memorise the residuals rather than learn the pattern — that is overfitting. The learning rate (typically 0.01–0.1) makes each tree contribute only a tiny correction, so the ensemble improves gradually without overshooting.

Analogy: Imagine adjusting a dial to hit the right temperature. Instead of cranking it all the way in one go (one big tree), you make hundreds of tiny nudges (many shallow trees), each one correcting what the last one got slightly wrong. You reach the right setting far more accurately.
Random ForestGradient Boosting (XGBoost)

Training speedFasterSlower

3.3 Neural Networks

Layers of interconnected "neurons" that learn to transform inputs into outputs through a process called backpropagation.

How learning happens — Backpropagation

  • Make a prediction → compare to real answer → measure the error (loss)
  • Work backwards through the network, adjusting each weight slightly
  • Repeat millions of times → network gradually learns the mapping

Deep Learning = neural networks with many hidden layers. Powers image recognition, speech, and large language models.

When to use

  • Large datasets (thousands to millions of examples)
  • Complex patterns: images, audio, text, video
  • When accuracy matters more than interpretability
⚠️ Watch out for: Need lots of data. Black box — hard to explain decisions. Computationally expensive to train (often needs GPU).

SECTION 04

🔍 Unsupervised Learning

No labels. No right answer. What structure exists naturally in this data?

4.1 K-Means Clustering

Divide your data into K groups (clusters) where points within a group are as similar as possible to each other and as different as possible from other groups.

How the algorithm works

  • Place K centroids randomly in the data space
  • Assign each data point to its nearest centroid (forming K clusters)
  • Move each centroid to the center (mean) of its assigned points
  • Repeat steps 2–3 until centroids stop moving (convergence)

Choosing K — The Elbow Method in detail

The elbow method plots Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS) for K = 1, 2, 3... and looks for where the improvement curve bends.

What is WCSS exactly? For every point in every cluster, measure how far it is from its cluster's centroid. Square that distance. Sum all of them up across all points and all clusters.

WCSS = Σ (over all clusters) Σ (over all points in cluster) distance(point, centroid)² Why square it? Same reason as linear regression — squaring penalises big errors more than small ones, so a cluster with a few very distant outliers gets a much higher WCSS than one with many slightly-off points. K = 1: one giant cluster, maximum WCSS K = N: every point is its own cluster, WCSS = 0 (useless) Goal: find K where adding one more cluster stops meaningfully reducing WCSS
How to read the elbow: The drop from K=1→2 is huge (you went from one blob to two meaningful groups). K=2→3 still a big drop. K=3→4 smaller. K=4→5 barely anything. The elbow at K=4 means: adding a 5th cluster gives you very little extra explanatory power — you're just splitting groups that were already reasonably coherent.
⚠️ The elbow is sometimes ambiguous — the curve bends gradually rather than sharply, and two people looking at the same plot might pick different K values. When that happens, use the Silhouette Score instead.

Choosing K — The Silhouette Score (more rigorous)

For every single data point, it asks: how well does this point fit in its own cluster, compared to the next best cluster?

For each point: a = average distance to all points in its own cluster (cohesion) b = average distance to all points in the nearest other cluster (separation) Silhouette(point) = (b − a) / max(a, b) Score = +1 → point is deep inside its cluster, far from others ✓ Score ≈ 0 → point is sitting on the boundary between two clusters Score = −1 → point is closer to another cluster than its own ✗ Average across all points → overall silhouette score for this K. Pick the K with the highest average silhouette score.
When to use which: Use the elbow method first — it is fast and visual. If the elbow is ambiguous, compute silhouette scores for the two or three candidate K values and pick the highest. In practice, domain knowledge often overrides both — "we want 3 segments because our team can run 3 campaigns."

Is K-means common in real life? Alternatives?

K-means is extremely common — fast, interpretable, scales well. But it has real limitations and alternatives exist for each one.

MethodKey ideaUse whenReal-world use
K-MeansAssign points to nearest centroid, minimise WCSSClusters are roughly round and similar in size. You know K roughly.Customer segmentation, image compression, document clustering
HDBSCANFind dense regions. Points in sparse areas = outliers (no forced assignment)Clusters have irregular shapes. You don't know K. Outliers are meaningful. Used in BERTopic.Geospatial clustering, anomaly detection, NLP topic modelling
HierarchicalBuild a tree of clusters (dendrogram). Cut the tree at any level to get K groups.You want to explore the cluster structure at multiple levels of granularity. Small-to-medium datasets.Bioinformatics, customer journey analysis, market research
GMM (Gaussian Mixture Models)Assumes data is a mix of Gaussian distributions. Gives soft assignments — each point has a probability of belonging to each cluster.Clusters overlap. You want probabilities, not hard labels. For example, a customer who is 60% "high value" and 40% "occasional."Signal processing, finance, probabilistic customer profiles
Marketing analytics rule of thumb: Start with K-means — it is fast, interpretable, and easy to explain to stakeholders. If clusters have weird shapes or you cannot justify a choice of K, switch to HDBSCAN. If you want probabilities rather than hard labels, use GMM. Hierarchical clustering is good when you want to present a "zoom in / zoom out" view of segments to a business team.

When to use it in marketing

  • Customer segmentation (the classic use case)
  • Any time you want to discover natural groupings before building predictive models
  • Reducing a long list of competitors or products into a manageable number of archetypes
⚠️ Watch out for: Must choose K in advance. Sensitive to outliers — one extreme customer can pull a centroid. Assumes clusters are roughly spherical. Always normalise your features first — K-means uses distance, so a feature measured in thousands (salary) will dominate one measured in ones (number of purchases) unless you scale them.

4.2 Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

When you have many features (say, 50 customer attributes), PCA finds the most important directions of variation and compresses data into fewer dimensions while retaining most information.

Analogy: You have a 3D cloud of data points. PCA is like shining a light from the angle that casts the largest, most informative shadow. That shadow is your compressed data — same essential information, fewer dimensions.

When to use

  • Too many features — reduce before feeding to another model
  • Visualisation: compress 50 dimensions to 2 to actually see the data
  • Remove redundant features carrying the same information
⚠️ Watch out for: New "principal components" are combinations of original features — no longer directly interpretable ("PC1" might mean "young + high spend + frequent visits combined").

📊 Marketing Models

Where data science meets real business decisions.

5.1 Customer Segmentation

Divide customers into groups with similar characteristics or behaviours to target them with different messages, offers, and experiences.

ApproachMethodExample
DemographicSimple groupingAge 18–25 vs 40–55
BehaviouralK-means, RFMFrequent buyers vs dormant customers
PredictiveML classificationLikely to churn vs likely to upgrade

RFM Analysis — a classic that maps directly to ML features

Recency — how recently did they buy? Frequency — how often do they buy? Monetary — how much do they spend? Score each customer 1–5 on each dimension. R=5, F=5, M=5 = your best customer. R=1, F=1, M=1 = nearly lost. → This scoring creates features you can feed directly into a churn or CLV model.

5.2 Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)

The total revenue you expect from a customer over their entire relationship with you. Knowing CLV tells you how much you can afford to spend acquiring a customer.

CLV = Average order value × Purchase frequency × Customer lifespan Example: €50 per visit × 4 visits/year × 3 years = €600 CLV If it costs €200 in ads to acquire them → 3x return ✓ If it costs €700 in ads to acquire them → bad deal ✗

ML models make this far more precise by predicting each component individually per customer — accounting for their specific behaviour, demographics, and product usage.

5.3 Conjoint Analysis

A technique to understand which product features customers value most — by asking them to choose between products with different combinations of attributes, then mathematically decomposing their preferences.

Example: Launching a new subscription. Do customers care more about price, features, or support quality?

Present choices:
• Plan A: €10/month, 5 features, email support
• Plan B: €20/month, 15 features, phone support

By analysing many such choices, you learn the weight each person places on each attribute — and can model willingness to pay for any combination.

5.4 Pricing & Promotion Analytics

Price elasticity

  • Elastic: 10% price increase → large drop in demand (luxury, competitive markets)
  • Inelastic: 10% price increase → small drop in demand (necessities, strong brands)

ML models estimate price elasticity per customer segment, enabling personalised pricing.

Uplift modelling

Don't just measure whether promoted customers bought more. Measure whether the promotion caused the purchase. Some customers would have bought anyway.

Uplift = P(buy | treated) − P(buy | not treated) The extra conversions caused by the promotion itself. This is the true incremental impact — what you actually paid for.

6.1 Why Text Matters for Marketing

Text data is everywhere — product reviews, social media, customer calls, live chat. It is unstructured, which makes it hard but also uniquely rich.

  • Monitor sentiment about your brand in real time
  • Problem discovery — what are customers complaining about?
  • Product innovation — what features do customers want?
  • Competitive positioning — how does your brand compare to competitors?
⚠️ Validity concern: Review data is never a representative sample. Amazon reviews skew heavily toward 5 stars. Only very happy and very unhappy customers tend to write. This means you cannot make causal claims from reviews — only associational ones. Comparisons between brands are more robust because the bias is likely similar across brands.

6.2 How Text Becomes Numbers — Text Representations

A model cannot read words. Everything must become a number. Here is how that thinking evolved:

MethodWhat it doesProblem
One-hot encodingEach word = sparse binary vector (one 1, rest 0s)No meaning captured — "good" and "great" look completely unrelated
Bag of WordsCount word occurrences in a documentIgnores word order — "not bad" and "bad not" look identical
Static embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe)Dense vectors capturing meaning from contextSame vector for "bank" whether it means finance or river
Contextual embeddings (BERT, GPT)Vector changes based on surrounding wordsComputationally expensive; needs GPU

6.3 Tokenisation — How LLMs Read Text

LLMs don't see words — they see tokens. A tokeniser splits text into sub-word units and maps them to integer IDs.

  • Token: A unit of text — can be a word, sub-word, character, or byte
  • BPE (Byte Pair Encoding): Iteratively merges most frequent character pairs. Used by GPT models
  • Why sub-words? Handles rare/new words without [UNK] token. Vocabulary stays manageable at 30K–100K tokens
  • Context window: The maximum number of tokens a model can process at once (e.g., 4K, 128K, even 400K)

6.4 Word & Sentence Embeddings

An embedding maps a word (or token, or document) to a dense vector of real numbers. Words with similar meanings get similar vectors — they sit close together in the embedding space.

J.R. Firth (1957): "A word is characterised by the company it keeps." Words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings. This is the entire insight behind Word2Vec and GloVe.

Static vs. Contextual Embeddings

Static (Word2Vec, GloVe)Contextual (BERT, GPT)
Same word, different meaning"bank" always has the same vector"bank" gets a different vector in financial vs. river context
TrainingWord co-occurrence statisticsMasked language modelling or next-token prediction
Best forSimple semantic tasks, brand positioningClassification, sentiment, complex NLP tasks

Cosine Similarity — Measuring Meaning

Once you have embeddings, you need a way to measure how similar two words are. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors (not their length). Range: −1 to +1.

cos(w_i, w_j) = (w_i · w_j) / (||w_i|| × ||w_j||) Example (hotel review embeddings): cos(great, good) = 0.89 → very similar ✓ cos(hotel, shower) = 0.46 → less similar ✓ cos(Hilton, Marriott) = 0.85 → competitors, same category ✓ Key: HIGH similarity = words used in similar contexts = semantically related. LOW similarity = different contexts = semantically distant.

Word Arithmetic & Brand Positioning

Because embeddings capture meaning as vectors, you can do arithmetic on them:

food + morning → breakfast-related words food + evening → dinner-related words King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen (classic example)
A running example (hotel brand positioning):

Step 1: Identify positioning attributes (clean, friendly, nice, excellent, cheap, rude)
Step 2: Identify competitors (Hilton, Sheraton, Hyatt, Marriott)
Step 3: Compute cosine similarity between each brand embedding and each attribute embedding

Results: Marriott has highest association with "clean" (0.535) and "excellent" (0.619). Hyatt most associated with "cheap" (0.493).

Important limitation: High similarity does NOT mean the brand IS clean. It means the word "clean" appears in similar review contexts as the brand name. The review sample may not be representative.

Pooling — From Token to Document Embedding

Token embeddings give each token its own vector. But documents have different numbers of tokens. To get one fixed-size vector per document, you pool:

MethodHowCaptures
Mean poolingAverage embeddings across all tokensOverall "average meaning" — most common choice
Max poolingMax value per dimension across tokensStrongest signal on each dimension
Min poolingMin value per dimensionUsually useless — not representative of document

6.5 Transformer Architecture — Inside LLMs

All modern LLMs are built on the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need," 2017). Understanding it is the key to knowing when and how to use these models.

The Attention Mechanism — How Tokens Talk to Each Other

Attention is what makes Transformers powerful. It allows each token to incorporate information from other tokens in the sequence, capturing context and meaning.

  • Query (Q): "What information am I looking for?" — a projection of the current token
  • Key (K): "What information do I contain?" — a projection of every other token
  • Value (V): "What information can I provide?" — the actual content to be extracted
  • Attention score: Q · K → softmax → weights → weighted sum of Vs → contextual output
Multi-head attention: Run this process multiple times in parallel, each with different learned Q/K/V matrices. Each "head" specialises in different patterns — one might capture syntax, another semantics. The outputs are concatenated and projected.

GPT (Decoder) vs BERT (Encoder)

BERT (Encoder-only)GPT (Decoder-only)
Training taskMasked Language Modelling — predict masked wordsNext-token prediction — predict what comes next
Attention directionBidirectional — sees ALL surrounding tokensUnidirectional (causal) — only sees preceding tokens
GenerationCannot generate textAutoregressive — generates one token at a time
Best forClassification, sentiment, search, embeddingsText generation, chatbots, summarisation
ExamplesBERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTaGPT-4, Llama, Claude
💡 For marketing analytics: If you want to classify reviews as positive/negative → BERT (sees the whole review at once, bidirectional). If you want to generate a product description → GPT. This distinction comes up constantly in practice.

6.6 Text Classification & Sentiment Analysis

Classifying text into categories (positive/negative, topic, intent) is one of the most common NLP applications in marketing.

Three approaches — ordered by data requirements

ApproachHowWhen to use
Zero-shot (embedding similarity)Embed label descriptions ("This is a positive review") and compare to document embedding via cosine similarity. Highest similarity wins.No labelled data at all. Accuracy ~83% on review data.
Frozen LLM + classifier headUse BERT as a fixed feature extractor. Train only a small classifier (e.g., logistic regression) on top of document embeddings.Small amount of labelled data. Very few parameters trained (~1,538 for binary classification).
Full fine-tuning (SFT)Train both the pretrained BERT AND a classification head jointly. Gradients flow through the entire model (~110M parameters).Sufficient labelled data. Best performance. Most computationally expensive.
Key distinction: Frozen approach = LLM is a fixed feature extractor, only the classifier learns. Fine-tuned approach = the LLM itself learns task-specific representations — BERT's internals shift to better capture sentiment patterns. A useful check is how many parameters are trained in each scenario.

6.7 Topic Modelling — Discovering What Customers Talk About

Rather than predicting predefined labels, topic modelling discovers what subjects are present in a corpus — without being told what to look for.

The BERTopic Pipeline (the main method)

  • Step 1 — Embed: Convert each sentence to a 384-dim vector using Sentence-BERT (SBERT)
  • Step 2 — Reduce: Apply UMAP to compress 384 dims to 5–10 dims while preserving local structure
  • Step 3 — Cluster: Apply HDBSCAN — finds clusters automatically without specifying K; assigns outliers to Topic −1
  • Step 4 — Label: Use c-TF-IDF to extract the most relevant and unique keywords per cluster
Key insight — split reviews into sentences first: A full hotel review covers multiple topics ("The room was clean but the breakfast was disappointing and parking was hard to find"). Clustering the whole review blends all topics. By splitting into sentences and clustering at sentence level, each cluster corresponds to one specific attribute customers discuss.

HDBSCAN vs K-Means

HDBSCAN (BERTopic default)K-Means
Number of clustersDetermined automaticallyMust specify K in advance
OutliersAssigns outliers to Topic −1Forces every document into a cluster

c-TF-IDF — Labelling Topics

Class-based TF-IDF weighs words by: (frequency in cluster) × log(average cluster size / total word frequency). A word is a good topic label if it is both relevant (frequent in the cluster) and unique (rare in other clusters).

From Topics to Marketing Insights — the Perceptual Map

The full pipeline for competitive brand analysis:

Step 1: Identify topics using BERTopic (sentence-level clustering) Step 2: Select relevant attributes from clusters (e.g., cleanliness, service, location) Step 3: Compute sentiment scores per sentence per attribute Step 4: Aggregate sentiment per brand per attribute → visualise as PCA biplot This gives a data-driven perceptual map where: - Attributes = vectors (arrows) - Brands = points - Position = sentiment profile across all attributes

6.8 Prompt Engineering

When using generative LLMs (GPT, Claude) for marketing tasks — sentiment labelling, review summarisation, brand analysis — the quality of your prompt determines the quality of the output.

Anatomy of a prompt

ComponentWhat it doesExample
PersonaSets the role and expertise"You are a marketing analyst specialising in hotel reviews"
InstructionThe actual task"Classify the sentiment of the following review"
ContextBackground the model needs"The review is from TripAdvisor, 5-star scale"
FormatSpecifies output structure"Respond only with: Positive, Neutral, or Negative"
DataThe actual input to process"The room was clean but service was slow"

Zero-shot, One-shot, Few-shot

  • Zero-shot: No examples — model relies entirely on training and instruction
  • One-shot: One example of input→output before the actual query
  • Few-shot: Two or more examples — often dramatically improves performance (in-context learning)

Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

Appending "Let's think step-by-step" to a prompt causes the model to show intermediate reasoning before its final answer. This distributes more computation over the reasoning process and often improves accuracy on complex tasks.

⚠️ Critical limitation: CoT is NOT a window into the model's internal computation. The verbalized reasoning steps do not faithfully reflect what actually happens inside the model. Research shows that changing the reasoning steps often doesn't change the answer — the conclusion can be driven by superficial features, not the stated logic. Never treat CoT output as a reliable explanation of model behaviour.

Temperature and Sampling

ParameterWhat it controlsMarketing use
TemperatureRandomness of token selection. Low = deterministic, high = creativeClassification tasks → low temp (0–0.2). Creative copy → high (0.7–1.0)
Top-kOnly sample from top K most likely tokensReduces wild outputs while maintaining variety

6.9 The "Lost in the Middle" Problem

LLMs perform best when relevant information is at the beginning or end of the context window — and worst when it is buried in the middle. This is because attention tends to be stronger at the edges (primacy and recency effects).

Practical implication: When constructing a long prompt with a product description, instructions, and review data — put your most important instructions at the very beginning or very end. Information placed in the middle is likely to be under-weighted by the model.

SECTION 07

📏 Model Evaluation & Selection

Building a model is 20% of the job. Knowing if it's actually good is the other 80%.

7.1 Train / Validation / Test Split

Never evaluate your model on the data it was trained on — it has already seen that data and will perform artificially well.

Cross-validation — a more reliable estimate

A single train/test split is fragile. Your performance number depends heavily on which examples happened to land in the test set. If they were easy, your model looks great. If hard, it looks terrible. You got lucky — not informed.

K-fold cross-validation fixes this by rotating the test set across K rounds:

  • Divide your data into K equal chunks (folds) — typically K=5 or K=10
  • Each round: train on K−1 folds, test on the remaining one
  • Repeat K times — every fold takes a turn as the test set exactly once
  • Average the K scores — this is your final performance estimate
Why more reliable? Every single data point gets tested exactly once across the K rounds — nothing is permanently left out, nothing is tested twice. If all K scores are close (82%, 79%, 85%...) you can trust the model is genuinely consistent. If they vary wildly (60%, 90%, 55%...) that is a signal: your model is unstable, probably overfitting.

When it matters most: Small datasets. If you only have 200 examples, a single 80/20 split gives you just 40 test points — statistically weak. K-fold tests every point.
⚠️ Trade-off: you train K models instead of 1, so it takes K times longer. K=5 or K=10 are the standard choices — they balance reliability against computational cost.
💡 Edge case worth knowing — Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV): K = N (number of data points). Train on every point except one, test on that one, repeat N times. Maximum reliability, but very slow on large datasets.

7.2 Classification Metrics

Accuracy alone is dangerously misleading. If 99% of transactions are not fraud, a model that always says "not fraud" has 99% accuracy but is completely useless.

MetricWhat it measuresUse when...
PrecisionOf all "Yes" predictions, how many were actually yes?False alarms are costly (spam filter — better to miss spam than block real emails)
RecallOf all actual "Yes" cases, how many did you find?Missing a case is costly (cancer screening — better to flag healthy patients than miss cancer)
F1 ScoreHarmonic mean of precision and recallYou need balance between both
ROC-AUCOverall model quality across all thresholdsComparing models: 0.5 = useless, 1.0 = perfect

7.4 Hyperparameter Tuning

Hyperparameters = settings you choose before training. They are not learned from data.

  • Number of trees in a Random Forest
  • K (number of clusters) in K-means
  • Learning rate in Gradient Boosting
  • Number of layers / neurons in a neural network

Grid Search: Try all combinations. Slow but thorough.
Random Search: Randomly sample combinations. Often finds good settings faster.

8.3 Storytelling with Data

A model's output means nothing to a stakeholder unless you translate it into a decision.

The 3-question framework

  • So what? — What does this finding mean for the business?
  • Now what? — What action should we take?
  • How sure are we? — What is the uncertainty or risk?
Example of good model communication:

"Our churn model shows a 73% probability that 2,300 customers will leave in the next 60 days, representing €1.1M in at-risk revenue. Targeting these customers with a retention campaign costing €50 each (€115K total) could save an estimated €600K based on historical retention rates. We recommend acting within 2 weeks."

8.4 A/B Testing — Establishing Causation

  • Randomly split users into Group A (control) and Group B (treatment)
  • Show A the old version, B the new version — keep everything else equal
  • Measure the difference in outcome (conversion, revenue, clicks)
  • Test whether the difference is statistically significant — not just random chance
p-value = probability you'd see this result by chance alone if there was no real effect p < 0.05 = less than 5% chance this is a fluke → statistically significant ✓ p > 0.05 = could easily be random noise → don't act on it ✗

Golden rule for ML: A logistic regression that works is better than a neural network you cannot explain or debug. Start simple. Earn the complexity.

Golden rule for NLP: More parameters ≠ better results. SBERT + logistic regression (F1=0.85) outperformed a task-specific fine-tuned Twitter-RoBERTa (F1=0.79) on hotel reviews. Domain match matters more than model size.
